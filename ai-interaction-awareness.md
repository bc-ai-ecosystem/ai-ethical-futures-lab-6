# 2. Empowering Individuals to Know When They Are Interacting with an AI System

## Challenge
Canadians interact with chatbots, voice assistants, customer-service systems and decision tools without always knowing an AI is involved. Deception risk is highest in high-stakes contexts (hiring, credit, health, personal relationships, financial transactions) and when systems are designed to appear human. Disclosure fatigue and overly broad mandates are real risks.

## Questions
* When and why is it most important for individuals to know they are interacting with an AI system? Are there contexts in which disclosure is unnecessary? What factors make an AI-use disclosure meaningful and effective?  
* Who in the AI value chain should be responsible for disclosing AI interactions, and why? Are existing market practices and legal frameworks sufficient? If not, where are the gaps and what actions should the Government take?

## Existing Practical Solutions
Explicit interface disclosures, system naming conventions and persistent UI indicators used by major providers. Ontario’s *Working for Workers Four Act* already requires AI disclosure in hiring. Canada’s Voluntary Code and forthcoming Safe Social Media Act address deceptive chatbots. Technical patterns include clear system prompts, voice-synthesis disclaimers and API-level flags.

## EU AI Act
Article 50(1) requires providers of AI systems intended to interact directly with natural persons to design and develop those systems so that the persons concerned are informed that they are interacting with an AI system, unless this is obvious from the circumstances of use or the system is authorised by law for law-enforcement or similar purposes. Commission guidelines clarify that this covers chatbots, virtual assistants and automated phone systems, and that AI agents capable of interacting with humans fall within scope.

Compliant providers build disclosure into the system architecture (e.g., persistent visual or verbal indicators that cannot easily be stripped by deployers). Deployers who present the interface must ensure the disclosure remains visible and understandable. The same high fines apply. The obligation is design-time for providers and presentation-time for deployers, creating clear allocation of responsibility.
