**AI Ethical Futures Lab 6 – Enhanced Meeting Summary**  
**Date:** Tuesday, 4 August 2026, 6:30 p.m.  
**Location:** Parker Street Studios, Vancouver  
**Theme:** AI Transparency, Power & Trust  
**Context:** Response to the Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada (ISED) public consultation on AI transparency (open ~23 July–23 September 2026; 23 survey questions). Facilitator took live notes and recorded the session. Notes and recording will be synthesized for possible group input.

The discussion was free-flowing rather than strictly sequential through the 23 questions. Participants repeatedly observed that the official questions may not fully capture the most urgent issues and that the first meta-question should be whether these are the right questions.

### 1. Session Purpose and Format
The group reviewed the five high-level theme areas from the ISED call and Kris’s preparatory framing. Consensus favoured full-group discussion (facilitated to ensure balanced speaking time) over breakouts, given the diverse expertise in the room. The goal was both practical feedback for a possible BC+AI / group submission and broader exploration of transparency, power, and trust.

### 2. Detecting & Labelling AI-Generated or AI-Modified Content
Strong agreement that knowing whether content was created or modified by AI would increase trust online, particularly for news, reviews, advertising, electoral material, and personal communications. Deepfakes were highlighted as amplifying fraud, non-consensual intimate imagery, and information-integrity risks.  

Key caveats:
- Binary “AI / not AI” framing is inadequate; hybrid and AI-assisted media are already common.
- Over-labelling risks signal fatigue; under-labelling enables deception.
- Counter-view: labels could create prejudice. In 20–30 years almost all content may contain some AI involvement; AI has lowered barriers to creative expression (e.g., music), so universal labelling might disadvantage legitimate uses.
- If AI content becomes the majority, it may be more useful to label “human-created / AI-free” instead.

### 3. Technical Provenance Solutions (C2PA and Alternatives)
C2PA (Content Credentials) was repeatedly endorsed as the emerging industry de-facto, vendor-neutral standard for embedding authenticity and provenance metadata into files. It can declare full AI generation, AI assistance, or human origin and is already referenced in the EU AI Act. Preferred over proprietary options such as Google’s SynthID.  

Practical questions remain: how credentials are verified, root-of-trust models (analogous to SSL), enforcement, and who audits them. All common disclosure methods (visible/invisible watermarks, disclaimers, provenance metadata) were viewed as useful.

### 4. Prevention, Education & Limits of Labelling Alone
Labelling was seen as necessary but insufficient (cigarette-package warning analogy). Multi-pronged approaches are required: prevention of harmful uses, public-health-style education, and upstream restrictions. Participants asked whether the consultation adequately addresses prevention. Skill training for average citizens on sovereign/local AI tools was also emphasised so people rely less solely on government.

### 5. Broader Contexts – Invisible & Systemic AI Use
Discussion expanded beyond visible generative media to:
- Algorithmic curation and ranking that already shapes what users see (what are these systems optimised for?).
- Generative AI inside electronic health records and clinical workflows (less visible to end-users).
- Background automated decision-making in bureaucratic and financial systems. A concrete personal example was shared of an unexplained inter-account money transfer attributed to the account-holder’s identity with no consent, no audit trail, and limited regulatory follow-up after complaints to multiple agencies.
- Public biometric capture (cameras tracking iris, breathing, heartbeat in stores, etc.) and the unresolved question of data ownership.

### 6. Consent, Privacy & Missing Governance
Quebec’s Law 25 real-time consent requirements were cited as a useful model currently missing from most provenance systems (including C2PA). Interoperability initiatives (e.g., health-data legislation) risk connecting data without corresponding consent and governance frameworks. Terms-of-service practices that allow platforms to claim and reuse user content and interaction data were widely criticised.

### 7. Accountability, Liability & the Human Element
Core questions: Who is responsible when systems cause harm—developers, deployers, adopting institutions, or government? Shared-responsibility models were favoured, yet assignment remains murky across complex chains.  

Strong emphasis on meaningful human-in-the-loop / human-override capability that is obvious and not a rubber stamp. Existing professional liability structures (e.g., medicine) offer partial templates. BC residential tenancy rules were suggested as an analogy: AI implementers should face enforceable minimum standards that override one-sided terms of service.

### 8. Defining “Serious AI Incidents” and Harm
Suggestions included:
- Harm to humans (threshold is culturally variable and intersectional).
- Any action that breaks existing law.
- Out-of-scope or uncontrolled behaviour by the system itself.
- Deepfakes of individuals as a hard line (each person should own their face, voice, likeness; unauthorised replication should be clearly unlawful).
- Scale of harm and transparency of training data (self-driving-car example: failure to detect certain humans because of biased training data).  

Individual-centric legal frameworks struggle with collective, cultural, corporate, or governmental harms (e.g., unjust credit scoring). China’s prohibition on AI impersonating humans (e.g., as a doctor or therapist) was noted as one existing hard boundary.

### 9. Data Ownership, Personal Control & Sovereign Alternatives
Widespread concern about who owns data generated by or about individuals. Calls for personal data stores/portability (user brings data to services), clearer ownership of biometric and behavioural data, and consumer-friendly self-sovereign technology. Decentralised approaches (AT Protocol / Bluesky), local/on-prem AI, and enterprise closed systems were all discussed as responses to big-tech capture. Funding allocation for AI transparency or sovereignty should reach SMEs, solopreneurs, artists, community organisations, and non-profits, not only large players.

### 10. Empowerment of End-Users vs. Top-Down Regulation Alone
Many stressed equipping non-experts (patients, citizens, clinicians under time pressure) to critically evaluate AI outputs and hallucinations rather than relying solely on slow government processes. Education and skill-building were framed as essential complements to regulation. At the same time, others insisted minimum enforceable standards remain necessary because most people will not migrate to decentralised platforms.

### 11. Critiques of the Consultation Process
Scepticism that the survey partly functions as consultation theatre or free consulting, that the questions are not the most urgent ones, and that feedback may be lightly processed. EU AI Act was viewed as a useful reference (especially given the EU’s leverage over large platforms) but not something to copy verbatim. Despite reservations, participants still saw value in responding because these are the questions currently on the table.

### 12. Community Next Steps & Logistics
- Facilitator will organise notes + recording (possibly with AI assistance) and share via group channels.
- Interest in a coordinated response on behalf of the group or BC+AI; volunteers offered help.
- Chat infrastructure: WhatsApp group exists; Discord bridge can be activated so both platforms share the same conversation.
- Desire for more action-oriented future sessions (building tools or infrastructure rather than only discussing). Suggestions included a September or October meeting (possibly at Mount Pleasant Community Centre), formation of a light steering committee, and leveraging the continuous influx of new participants.

The conversation remained wide-ranging and did not answer every official survey question in sequence, but the human notes and transcript together surface consistent priorities: meaningful provenance (C2PA + consent), prevention alongside labelling, clear accountability and human override, data ownership and sovereignty, user empowerment through education, and enforceable minimum standards.
