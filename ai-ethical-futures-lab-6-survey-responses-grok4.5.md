**Q-1.** Would it help you trust what you see online if you could tell whether something was created by AI or by a person? When and for what kinds of content (e.g., image, video, audio) is it most important to know if something was created or modified by AI?  
**A-1.** Yes — it would help trust. Most important for images, video, audio, news, reviews, advertising, electoral content, and personal communications. The “how” (and degree of AI involvement / hybrid nature) matters more than a simple binary. Prevention and education are also needed alongside labelling. Labels risk prejudice or fatigue once AI involvement becomes nearly universal.

**Q-2.** What would best help Canadians determine when content is AI-generated (e.g., invisible or visible watermarks, disclaimers, provenance metadata)?  
**A-2.** Provenance metadata via open standards such as C2PA (Content Credentials) was strongly favoured as a vendor-neutral way to declare full AI generation, AI assistance, or human origin. Visible/invisible watermarks and disclaimers were also seen as useful. If AI content becomes the majority, labelling “human-created / AI-free” may be more practical.

**Q-3.** Who in the AI value chain (developers, deployers, or others) should be responsible for providing transparency around AI-generated content? Why?  
**A-3.** Shared responsibility across the value chain (developers, deployers, and institutions that adopt the systems). Institutions using AI for decisions (e.g., banks, healthcare) must also be accountable. Meaningful human-in-the-loop / override capability is essential; pure “the computer did it” is insufficient.

**Q-4.** Do existing market practices, technical tools, and legal frameworks make it easy enough to know when content is AI-generated? If not, where do gaps remain and what actions (e.g., regulatory measures, guidance and codes of conduct, standards and technical solutions, research and development, literacy initiatives, procurement requirements) do you think the Government should take?  
**A-4.** No — existing practices and tools leave significant gaps (verification of watermarks, consent, auditability of background decisions, prevention). Suggested actions: adopt/require open standards such as C2PA, reference useful elements of the EU AI Act without copying verbatim, enforce minimum standards that override one-sided terms of service (BC rental-law analogy), literacy/education initiatives, and ensure funding reaches SMEs, artists, community organisations and non-profits.

**Q-5.** When and why is it most important to know that you are interacting with an AI system? Are there contexts where you don’t need to know that you’re interacting with AI?  
**A-5.** Every time, until genuine trust exists. Especially important for banks, bureaucratic/financial systems, healthcare, and any context where decisions affect rights, money, health or identity. No contexts were identified where disclosure is unnecessary under current conditions.

**Q-6.** What factors make a disclosure of AI use meaningful and effective for the user?  
**A-6.** Disclosure must convey how AI was used (not just that it was used), include consent/provenance information, make human override capability clear and real (not a rubber stamp), and avoid fatigue (progressive disclosure or tiny icons suggested). Source of data/models and training-data transparency also matter.

**Q-7.** Who in the AI value chain (developers, deployers, or others) should be responsible for providing transparency around AI interactions? Why?  
**A-7.** Same shared responsibility as question 3: developers for system-level transparency, deployers and adopting institutions for real-world use and decision contexts. End-users and affected individuals must be able to obtain explanations and remedies.

**Q-8.** Are existing market practices and legal frameworks sufficient to support transparency around AI interactions? If not, where do gaps remain and what actions (e.g., regulatory measures, guidance and codes of conduct, standards and technical solutions, research and development, literacy initiatives, procurement requirements) do you think the Government should take?  
**A-8.** Not sufficient. Gaps include lack of real-time consent (Quebec Law 25 cited as model), weak audit trails, unclear liability, and missing prevention. Actions needed: regulatory minimum standards, open technical standards, literacy, and enforceable human-in-the-loop requirements.

**Q-9.** What kinds of information about AI would you like to know in order to help you make informed choices about when and how to use AI? When and for which types of AI products is this information most important to you?  
**A-9.** When and how AI is used, degree of involvement (hybrid vs full), training-data characteristics and limitations, existence of human override, data ownership/consent status, and source of the system. Highest priority for high-stakes domains (health, finance, identity, elections, legal/credit decisions).

**Q-10.** What kind of information about AI do businesses need to adopt AI safely and responsibly? Do businesses currently have access to the information that they need? How can the Government best balance the need to increase transparency (including input transparency) against the need to protect confidential business information?  
**A-10.** Clarity on governance, liability allocation, training-data limitations, and risk controls. Access is currently incomplete. Balancing transparency against confidential business information was not discussed in depth → partial / limited information only.

**Q-11.** Who in the AI value chain (developers, deployers, or others) is best placed to provide what information about AI systems, how they are created, and their capabilities and limitations?  
**A-11.** Developers are best placed for information on creation, capabilities and limitations; deployers and adopting organisations for real-world use, context and outcomes. Shared responsibility overall.

**Q-12.** Are existing disclosure practices and legal frameworks sufficient to provide the information that users and other stakeholders need? If not, where do gaps remain and what actions (e.g., regulatory measures, guidance and codes of conduct, standards and technical solutions, research and development, literacy initiatives, procurement requirements) do you think the Government should take?  
**A-12.** Existing practices and frameworks are insufficient. Gaps remain in consent, auditability, collective harms and enforcement. Needed actions include standards (C2PA), regulation of minimum requirements, literacy initiatives and clearer liability rules.

**Q-13.** Do the public and the Government have enough information about AI risks, incidents, and potential future harms? What types of information related to AI use and risk trends would be most useful so that Canadians and the Government can proactively address AI-related risks and harms?  
**A-13.** No. Most useful information: causes and consequences of incidents, training-data transparency and bias, risk trends (including collective/cultural harms), existence of human overrides, and systemic failures (e.g., unexplained automated financial decisions).

**Q-14.** How should a serious AI incident be defined, and what information would be most useful to better understand the causes and consequences, as well as the mitigation, of these incidents? How could reporting requirements be structured to encourage proactive disclosure of AI incidents and also address concerns around confidentiality and liability?  
**A-14.** Possible definitions: any incident causing harm to humans (low threshold, culturally variable), any action that breaks existing law, out-of-scope or uncontrolled system behaviour, or deepfakes of individuals. Useful information: causes, consequences, mitigation steps, and whether human override was available. Reporting should encourage proactive disclosure while addressing confidentiality and liability; shared accountability across the chain was emphasised.

**Q-15.** Which individuals or actors (e.g., developers, deployers) are best placed to provide the most relevant information? Who (e.g., regulatory bodies, nonprofits, others) should collect this information and what should be done with it?  
**A-15.** Developers and deployers are best placed to supply the technical and operational information. Regulatory bodies (or independent entities) should collect it for accountability, learning and public transparency.

**Q-16.** Are existing incident reporting frameworks in Canada (e.g., for consumer products, medical devices, transportation) sufficient to address serious incidents involving AI? If not, where do gaps remain and what actions (e.g., regulatory measures, guidance and codes of conduct, standards and technical solutions, research and development, literacy initiatives, procurement requirements) do you think the Government should take? Are there emerging international best practices that Canada could adapt or interoperate with?  
**A-16.** Existing Canadian frameworks (privacy, consumer, etc.) are not sufficient for AI-specific issues such as collective harm, training-data opacity and autonomous decision-making. Gaps in auditability and enforcement. Canada could adapt useful elements of the EU AI Act and open standards such as C2PA, and learn from hard limits in other jurisdictions (e.g., China’s prohibition on AI impersonating humans in certain roles).

**Q-17.** Are you concerned about the use of AI agents? In what contexts is it most important to know when an AI agent is in use? Why?  
**A-17.** Yes, concern was expressed (autonomous decision-making, deepfakes, unexplained actions). Most important in high-stakes contexts (finance, healthcare, identity, legal/credit decisions) because of lack of consent, audit trails and human override.

**Q-18.** Who should be responsible for disclosing the use of AI agents? What kinds of information should they be recording regarding the activities of AI agents, and what kinds of disclosures should they be making, and to whom?  
**A-18.** Deployers and institutions that put agents into use. They should record actions, consent status, overrides and outcomes, and disclose to affected individuals and relevant regulators. Pure “the system did it” is unacceptable.

**Q-19.** What kinds of information do businesses need in order to confidently and safely use AI agents? Do businesses currently have access to the information that they need about AI agents?  
**A-19.** n/a (limited discussion of business-specific information needs for agents).

**Q-20.** Are existing technical solutions, market practices, and legal frameworks sufficient to support transparency around agentic AI systems? If not, where do gaps remain and what actions (e.g., regulatory measures, guidance and codes of conduct, standards and technical solutions, research and development, literacy initiatives, procurement requirements) do you think the Government should take?  
**A-20.** Not sufficient. Gaps in transparency of agent actions, consent, auditability and liability. Needed: regulatory requirements for human-in-the-loop, open standards, literacy and enforceable minimum standards.

**Q-21.** Given the areas discussed here, when is AI transparency most important, and are there any areas where action would be premature? Are there other areas that should be considered for action to enhance AI transparency?  
**A-21.** Most important for high-stakes decisions, identity/deepfakes, background automated systems, elections and any context affecting rights or money. Action on provenance (C2PA), accountability, consent and human override is not premature. Additional areas raised: data ownership, funding reaching SMEs/community actors, and prevention alongside labelling.

**Q-22.** How should any measures account for the circumstances of small and medium-sized enterprises?  
**A-22.** Measures and funding must reach SMEs, solopreneurs, artists, community organisations and non-profits rather than only large players. Support consumer-friendly, self-sovereign and local AI options.

**Q-23.** How should Canada ensure coherence between any federal measures and provincial, territorial, and international frameworks? What approaches in other jurisdictions could best inform a Canadian consideration of this issue?  
**A-23.** Ensure coherence with provincial frameworks (Quebec Law 25 real-time consent, BC privacy rules) and international ones. Useful references: EU AI Act (leverage over large platforms, without verbatim copy) and open standards such as C2PA. Hard limits used elsewhere (e.g., China on impersonation) can inform Canadian boundaries.

**Q-24.** How can Canada design measures to best account for the ongoing evolution in AI technologies and their use?  
**A-24.** Prefer principle-based and standards-based approaches (open provenance, human override, consent, accountability) that can evolve with technology rather than rigid, technology-specific rules. Combine with ongoing literacy, review mechanisms and support for local/sovereign alternatives.
